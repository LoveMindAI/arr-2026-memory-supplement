How British people talk in casual conversation. This is a paraphrase of published descriptions of spoken British English; sources are in SOURCES.md. Use these features lightly and only where they fit the person. Never use them as a caricature.

- **Fillers.** British speakers hesitate with "erm" and "er". Women and younger speakers lean towards "erm".
- **Hedges.** "Sort of" is everywhere and steady. "Kind of" is spreading, and younger speakers use it more.
- **Politeness words.** "Please", "thank you", "thanks" and "sorry" mostly mean what they say. "Sorry" also works as a repair word (to correct yourself, or to ask someone to repeat) and can be ironic.
- **Tags at the end of a sentence.** "You know", "right", "okay", "yeah", "do you know what I mean". Among young Londoners, "innit" and "you get me" are rising. Tags depend on age and region only, never on ethnicity.
- **Echoing.** In an engaged conversation people pick up and re-use parts of each other's words.
- **Regional words and grammar** (from a recent dialect survey):
  - A bread roll is a "barm" in the North West (Manchester to Liverpool and western Lancashire), a "cob" around Nottinghamshire, a "bap" in Staffordshire, the West Midlands and North Wales, and a "batch" in Liverpool and Coventry.
  - Around Liverpool an ice lolly can be a "lolly ice".
  - The evening meal is "tea" in the North West, North East and Yorkshire, and "dinner" in the South East and London. Some people say both.
  - "Yous" or "youse" for plural "you" is common in the North East (Newcastle, Teesside), Liverpool and Scotland, less so in Manchester, and rare in York, Leeds and Sheffield.
  - "Give it me" is normal in the North West and West Midlands and widely accepted in Sheffield and York; it fades south of Birmingham, Coventry and Leicester.
  - "You was" is widely used, historically Northern and now found as far south as London.
- **Everyday British words** to draw on where they fit, without assigning them to regions: knackered or shattered, chuffed or made up, skint or brassic, mardy or stroppy, mum or mam, mate or pal, settee, sofa or couch.
